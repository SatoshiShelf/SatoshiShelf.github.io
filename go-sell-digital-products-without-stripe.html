Stripe is convenient. It is also a permission slip. If you sell digital downloads to buyers who do not have a usable card, or you do not want a processor deciding whether your product is allowed, Bitcoin checkout is a real alternative. It is not a drop-in replacement.

Card processors solve a hard problem: they let strangers pay each other without trusting each other. That convenience has a price. Accounts can be frozen, payouts can be delayed, categories can be restricted, and buyers in some countries cannot complete a card checkout at all.

Direct Bitcoin payments remove the processor from the path. They also remove the safety net, the dispute layer, and the familiar checkout. The right choice depends on what you sell and who buys it.

Why sellers look elsewhere

  • Access. A buyer without a usable card can still pay on-chain.
  • Control. No processor can freeze a self-custody wallet or change your payout schedule.
  • Finality. A confirmed Bitcoin payment cannot be charged back through the card network.
  • Privacy. No card number, no customer account, and no need to store a buyer database.
  • Global reach. A wallet works across borders in a way that a merchant account often does not.

None of those benefits are absolute. Each one has an operational cost, and the cost is usually paid in support time rather than a monthly fee.

Who it fits

Good fit

Digital downloads with instant delivery, a small catalog, and buyers who already use Bitcoin or cannot use cards. You are willing to test the payment flow and answer edge-case support.

Poor fit

Subscriptions, high-volume mainstream retail, buyers who expect cards, and stores that cannot support a failed payment without a help desk. A card processor is still the simpler default.

If your goal is to test demand, a marketplace or a hosted gateway is faster. If your goal is a durable direct store, own the checkout and the files. The two can coexist: use a marketplace for discovery and your own store for repeat buyers.

The trade-offs

You own the edge cases

Underpayments, late payments, duplicate transaction IDs, wrong-network transfers, and buyers who close the tab before the file unlocks. A card processor absorbs some of this. A direct store does not.

You own the tax question

Gumroad is a merchant of record and handles sales tax for its sellers. A direct store is a direct sale. If you sell internationally, tax and consumer-law obligations are yours to understand.

You own the trust signal

A buyer who has never heard of you may hesitate before sending irreversible money. Clear product pages, a real support channel, a sample, and a precise delivery promise do more than a “powered by Bitcoin” badge.

You own the price risk

If you quote in Bitcoin and the price moves before the buyer pays, you carry the difference. Quote in fiat, lock the satoshi amount for a short window, and make the expiry visible.

A practical migration

  1. Pick one product. Not the whole catalog. Choose a product with a clear price and an instant delivery path.
  2. Create a self-custody wallet. Back it up, test recovery, and decide how you will generate receiving addresses.
  3. Build or buy checkout. Static page plus serverless verification, BTCPay Server, or a hosted gateway. Keep the first version small.
  4. Write the policy. Delivery, refunds, underpayments, support, and what data you do or do not collect.
  5. Test with real money. A tiny real payment catches more than a testnet transaction. Test the unhappy paths too.
  6. Add a card rail later if you need one. Keep the two flows separate so a failed card payment cannot leave a Bitcoin order in limbo.

Do not lead with “no Stripe.” Lead with the buyer’s problem: “Pay without a card,” “Download instantly,” or “No account required.” The payment rail is a feature, not the product.

FAQ

Can I sell digital products without a payment processor?
Yes. A direct Bitcoin payment goes from the buyer’s wallet to yours; the blockchain is the settlement layer. You still need a way to quote a price, verify the transaction, and deliver the file, but you do not need a merchant account or a card processor.
Will I lose buyers if I do not take cards?
Some, yes. Card checkout is familiar and works for buyers who already have cards. Bitcoin checkout wins with buyers who have no usable card, do not want to share card data, or are in countries your card processor handles poorly. A hybrid store can serve both.
Is it cheaper than Stripe?
The platform percentage can be lower because there is no card processor in the path. Network fees and hosting still exist, and your time has a cost. For small digital products, the bigger win is often access and control rather than a dramatic fee difference.
Do I have to handle sales tax myself?
If you sell directly, you are generally responsible for understanding your tax obligations. A marketplace or merchant-of-record can handle some of that for you, but it also takes a cut and controls the checkout. Do not assume Bitcoin removes tax complexity.
What about chargebacks and refunds?
On-chain Bitcoin payments do not have card chargebacks. That does not mean every sale is final in the eyes of the law. Publish a clear refund policy and handle genuine mistakes like wrong-amount payments or duplicate orders.
Can I start with one product?
Yes. One product, one wallet, one static page, one verification endpoint. Test the full path with a real payment before you add categories, discounts, or a second payment rail.
